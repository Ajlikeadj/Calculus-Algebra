Review of Curve Tracing

Curve tracing is the systematic process of sketching the qualitative shape of a curve from its equation, without plotting dozens of points. The goal is to identify essential features — symmetry, intercepts, asymptotes, loops, cusps, and extent — and combine them into an accurate sketch.

1.1 Cartesian Curves

Standard Checklist for Cartesian Curves $y = f(x)$ or $F(x,y)=0$

  1. Symmetry — Even $f$: symmetric about $y$-axis. Odd $f$: symmetric about origin. $f(-x) = -f(x)$: antisymmetric. For $F(x,y)=0$: replace $(x,y)\to(-x,y)$ or $(x,-y)$ or $(-x,-y)$.
  2. Intercepts — Set $y=0$ for $x$-intercepts; set $x=0$ for $y$-intercepts.
  3. Asymptotes — Vertical: values $x=a$ where $y\to\pm\infty$. Horizontal: $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty} f(x) = L$. Oblique: $y = mx+c$ where $m = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{f(x)}{x}$, $c = \lim_{x\to\infty}[f(x)-mx]$.
  4. Tangents at origin — Lowest-degree terms of $F(x,y)$ give tangent directions.
  5. Region of existence — Find $x$-values where $y^2 \geq 0$ (for implicit curves). Note forbidden regions.
  6. Monotonicity & Extrema — Sign of $dy/dx$; critical points.
  7. Special points — Cusps ($dy/dx$ undefined), nodes (curve crosses itself), inflection points.

Oblique Asymptote Method (Newton–McLaurin)

For a polynomial curve $F(x,y) = 0$, substitute $y = mx + c$ and collect the highest-degree terms:

$$\phi_n(m) = 0 \quad \text{(gives slopes } m\text{)}, \qquad c = -\frac{\phi_{n-1}(m)}{\phi_n'(m)}$$

where $\phi_k(m)$ is formed by putting $y=m$ in the degree-$k$ homogeneous part of $F$.

Example 1 — Semi-cubical Parabola: $y^2 = x^3$

  • Symmetry: Replace $y\to -y$: equation unchanged ⟹ symmetric about $x$-axis.
  • Intercepts: Only at origin $(0,0)$.
  • Asymptotes: None (curve extends to $+\infty$ as $x\to+\infty$).
  • Existence: $x^3 = y^2 \geq 0 \Rightarrow x \geq 0$. Curve lies entirely in right half-plane.
  • Tangent at origin: Lowest-degree terms: $y^2 = 0 \Rightarrow y = 0$ (the $x$-axis is the tangent — a cusp).
  • Shape: $y = \pm x^{3/2}$; the upper branch rises steeply, meeting the $x$-axis with a cusp at $O$.

Example 2 — $y^2 = x(1-x)^2$

  • Symmetry: About $x$-axis ($y\to-y$ invariant).
  • Intercepts: $x=0$: $y=0$. $x=1$: $y=0$. So the curve passes through $(0,0)$ and $(1,0)$.
  • Existence: $x(1-x)^2 \geq 0 \Rightarrow x \geq 0$ (since $(1-x)^2 \geq 0$ always). No upper bound on $x$.
  • Tangent at origin: Leading terms: $y^2 - x = 0 \Rightarrow y^2 = x$, i.e. two tangents $y = \pm\sqrt{x}$ — a node.
  • Tangent at $(1,0)$: $x=1$ gives $y^2 = 0$, so the curve touches $x=1$ — a cusp.
  • Large $x$: $y \approx \pm x(1-x) \to \pm(x - x^2)$; curve re-enters the region $x>1$ forming a loop that closes at $(1,0)$.

Example 3 — Folium of Descartes: $x^3 + y^3 = 3axy$, $a>0$

  • Symmetry: Interchanging $x \leftrightarrow y$ leaves the equation unchanged — symmetric about $y = x$.
  • Intercept: Passes through the origin.
  • Tangent at origin: Degree-2 terms: $3axy = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0$ or $y = 0$. Two tangents — a node.
  • Oblique asymptote: Substitute $y = mx + c$. The degree-3 part: $\phi_3(m) = 1 + m^3$. Setting $\phi_3(m)=0$ gives $m = -1$. Then $c = -\frac{\phi_2(-1)}{\phi_3'(-1)} = -\frac{-3a}{3(-1)^2} = a$. Asymptote: $\boxed{x + y + a = 0}$.
  • Loop: Using parametric form $x = \frac{3at}{1+t^3}$, $y = \frac{3at^2}{1+t^3}$, the loop lies in the first quadrant for $0 < t < \infty$.

Example 4 — $y = \dfrac{x}{1+x^2}$

  • Symmetry: $f(-x) = -f(x)$ — odd function, symmetric about origin.
  • Intercepts: Only at $(0,0)$.
  • Asymptote: $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty} y = 0$, so $y = 0$ is a horizontal asymptote.
  • Extrema: $y' = \frac{(1+x^2) - 2x^2}{(1+x^2)^2} = \frac{1-x^2}{(1+x^2)^2}$. Zeros at $x = \pm 1$. Max at $(1, \tfrac12)$, min at $(-1, -\tfrac12)$.
  • Inflections: $y'' = 0$ at $x = 0, \pm\sqrt{3}$.

1.2 Polar Curves

Symmetry Tests for Polar Curves $r = f(\theta)$

  • About polar axis ($x$-axis): Replace $\theta \to -\theta$; equation unchanged.
  • About $\theta = \pi/2$ ($y$-axis): Replace $\theta \to \pi - \theta$; equation unchanged.
  • About pole: Replace $r \to -r$ (or $\theta \to \theta + \pi$); equation unchanged.

Additional steps: find values where $r = 0$ (tangents to pole), $r$ is max, and whether the curve is bounded.

Example 5 — Cardioid: $r = a(1 + \cos\theta)$, $a > 0$

  • Symmetry: $\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$ — symmetric about polar axis.
  • Range of $r$: $0 \leq r \leq 2a$ (min at $\theta=\pi$, max at $\theta=0$).
  • Key points: $(2a, 0)$, $(a, \pi/2)$, $(0, \pi)$, $(a, 3\pi/2)$.
  • At pole: $r = 0$ when $\theta = \pi$; $dr/d\theta = -a\sin\pi = 0$ — a cusp at the pole.
  • Area: $A = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi} r^2\,d\theta = \frac{3\pi a^2}{2}$.
  • Shape: A heart-shaped curve touching the origin with a cusp on the left.

Example 6 — Four-Leaf Rose: $r = a\cos 2\theta$

  • Symmetry: Both $\theta\to-\theta$ and $\theta\to\pi-\theta$ — symmetric about both axes.
  • Petals: $r = 0$ when $2\theta = \pi/2, 3\pi/2, 5\pi/2, 7\pi/2$, i.e. $\theta = \pi/4, 3\pi/4, 5\pi/4, 7\pi/4$ — four petals.
  • Max $r = a$ at $\theta = 0, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2$.
  • Petal tips along the axes; petals have length $a$.

Example 7 — Lemniscate of Bernoulli: $r^2 = a^2\cos 2\theta$

  • Symmetry: About both axes and the pole.
  • Existence: $r^2 \geq 0 \Rightarrow \cos 2\theta \geq 0 \Rightarrow \theta \in [-\pi/4, \pi/4] \cup [3\pi/4, 5\pi/4]$.
  • Shape: Two symmetric loops (like a figure-eight) centred at the origin, each loop extending to $r = a$ along the $x$-axis.
  • Area of one loop: $A = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4} a^2\cos 2\theta\,d\theta = \frac{a^2}{2}$.

1.3 Parametric Curves

For $x = g(t)$, $y = h(t)$: find $t$ at key points (cusps, loops, extrema), note $dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt)$, and detect cusps when $dx/dt = dy/dt = 0$ simultaneously.

Example 8 — Cycloid: $x = a(t - \sin t)$, $y = a(1 - \cos t)$

  • Cusps: $\frac{dx}{dt} = a(1-\cos t) = 0$ and $\frac{dy}{dt} = a\sin t = 0$ both vanish at $t = 0, 2\pi, 4\pi, \ldots$, giving cusps at $(0,0),(2\pi a, 0),(4\pi a,0),\ldots$
  • Max height: $y = 2a$ at $t = \pi$, corresponding to $x = \pi a$ (apex of each arch).
  • Slope: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\sin t}{1-\cos t} = \cot(t/2)$. At $t=\pi$: slope $= 0$ (horizontal tangent at top). At cusps: slope $\to \infty$ (vertical).
  • Shape: Repeated arches of height $2a$ and period $2\pi a$, each cusp on the $x$-axis.
  • Arc length of one arch: $L = \int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{(dx/dt)^2+(dy/dt)^2}\,dt = 8a$.
  • Area under one arch: $A = \int_0^{2\pi a} y\,dx = 3\pi a^2$.

Curvature

Geometric Definition

As a point $P$ moves along a curve, the tangent line rotates. The curvature $\kappa$ at $P$ is the rate of rotation of the tangent with respect to arc length:

$$\kappa = \left|\frac{d\psi}{ds}\right|$$

where $\psi$ is the inclination of the tangent and $s$ is arc length. High curvature means the curve bends sharply; zero curvature means straight.

2.1 Curvature in Cartesian Form

Using $\tan\psi = dy/dx = y_1$ and arc length element $ds = \sqrt{1+y_1^2}\,dx$:

$$\frac{d\psi}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\arctan(y_1) = \frac{y_2}{1+y_1^2}$$ $$\frac{d\psi}{ds} = \frac{d\psi/dx}{ds/dx} = \frac{y_2/(1+y_1^2)}{\sqrt{1+y_1^2}} = \frac{y_2}{(1+y_1^2)^{3/2}}$$

Curvature — Cartesian Form

$$\kappa = \frac{|y_2|}{(1+y_1^2)^{3/2}}, \qquad y_1 = \frac{dy}{dx},\quad y_2 = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$$

Curvature of a Circle

For a circle of radius $R$: $y_1^2 + (y-k)^2 = R^2$ gives, after differentiation, $\kappa = 1/R$ everywhere. Thus curvature is uniform and equals the reciprocal of the radius.

2.2 Signed vs. Unsigned Curvature

The signed curvature is $\kappa_s = y_2/(1+y_1^2)^{3/2}$: positive when bending left (concave up), negative when bending right (concave down). The unsigned curvature is $\kappa = |\kappa_s|$, used for the radius of curvature $R = 1/\kappa$.

2.3 Curvature — Parametric Form

With $x = x(t)$, $y = y(t)$, let dots denote $d/dt$:

$$y_1 = \frac{\dot{y}}{\dot{x}}, \qquad y_2 = \frac{\dot{x}\ddot{y} - \dot{y}\ddot{x}}{\dot{x}^3}$$

Curvature — Parametric Form

$$\kappa = \frac{|\dot{x}\ddot{y} - \dot{y}\ddot{x}|}{(\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2)^{3/2}}$$

2.4 Curvature — Polar Form

For $r = f(\theta)$ write $r_1 = dr/d\theta$, $r_2 = d^2r/d\theta^2$. Converting to parametric with $\theta$ as parameter:

$$\dot{x} = r_1\cos\theta - r\sin\theta, \quad \dot{y} = r_1\sin\theta + r\cos\theta$$ $$\dot{x}^2+\dot{y}^2 = r^2 + r_1^2$$ $$\dot{x}\ddot{y} - \dot{y}\ddot{x} = r^2 + 2r_1^2 - rr_2$$

Curvature — Polar Form

$$\kappa = \frac{|r^2 + 2r_1^2 - rr_2|}{(r^2+r_1^2)^{3/2}}$$

2.5 Physical Interpretation

Physical Meaning

In mechanics, when a particle moves along a curved path at speed $v$, the centripetal acceleration needed to maintain the path is $a_n = v^2 \kappa$. Designers of roads and railways use curvature to determine the maximum safe speed through a bend.

In optics, the curvature of a mirror or lens surface directly determines its focal length via $f = R/2$.

Example 1 — Curvature of the Parabola $y = x^2$

$y_1 = 2x$, $y_2 = 2$.

$$\kappa = \frac{|2|}{(1+4x^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{2}{(1+4x^2)^{3/2}}$$

At the vertex $(0,0)$: $\kappa = 2$, so $R = 1/2$. As $x \to \infty$, $\kappa \to 0$ — the parabola straightens out.

Example 2 — Curvature of an Ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$ (Parametric)

$x = a\cos t$, $y = b\sin t$. Then $\dot{x} = -a\sin t$, $\dot{y} = b\cos t$, $\ddot{x} = -a\cos t$, $\ddot{y} = -b\sin t$.

$$\dot{x}\ddot{y} - \dot{y}\ddot{x} = (-a\sin t)(-b\sin t) - (b\cos t)(-a\cos t) = ab\sin^2 t + ab\cos^2 t = ab$$ $$\dot{x}^2+\dot{y}^2 = a^2\sin^2 t + b^2\cos^2 t$$ $$\kappa = \frac{ab}{(a^2\sin^2 t + b^2\cos^2 t)^{3/2}}$$

At end of major axis ($t=0$): $\kappa = ab/b^3 = a/b^2$. At end of minor axis ($t=\pi/2$): $\kappa = ab/a^3 = b/a^2$.

Example 3 — Curvature of $y = \sin x$

$y_1 = \cos x$, $y_2 = -\sin x$.

$$\kappa = \frac{|\sin x|}{(1+\cos^2 x)^{3/2}}$$

Max curvature at $x = \pi/2$ (peak): $\kappa_{\max} = 1/1^{3/2} = 1$, $R_{\min} = 1$. Zero curvature at $x = 0, \pi$ (inflection points).

Radius of Curvature — Cartesian Form

Radius of Curvature

$$R = \frac{1}{\kappa} = \frac{(1+y_1^2)^{3/2}}{|y_2|}, \qquad y_1 = \frac{dy}{dx},\quad y_2 = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$$

3.1 Derivation

We derived $\kappa = y_2/(1+y_1^2)^{3/2}$ in Section 2. Since $R = 1/\kappa$:

$$R = \frac{(1+y_1^2)^{3/2}}{|y_2|}$$

The absolute value ensures $R > 0$. Geometrically, $R$ is the radius of the osculating circle — the unique circle that best approximates the curve at the given point.

3.2 Implicit Curves $F(x,y) = 0$

Differentiate $F(x,y)=0$ implicitly. Let $F_x = \partial F/\partial x$, $F_y = \partial F/\partial y$, etc.

$$y_1 = -\frac{F_x}{F_y}$$ $$y_2 = -\frac{F_{xx}F_y^2 - 2F_{xy}F_xF_y + F_{yy}F_x^2}{F_y^3}$$

Radius of Curvature for Implicit Curve

$$R = \frac{(F_x^2 + F_y^2)^{3/2}}{|F_{xx}F_y^2 - 2F_{xy}F_xF_y + F_{yy}F_x^2|}$$

3.3 Newton's Formula at the Origin

Newton's Formula

If the curve passes through the origin with the $x$-axis as tangent (so $y_1(0)=0$), then:

$$R_O = \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{x^2}{2y} \quad \text{or} \quad R_O = \frac{1}{y_2(0)}$$

This is derived by Taylor-expanding $y$ near $x = 0$: $y = \frac{x^2}{2R} + \cdots$

Example 1 — $y = x^2$

$y_1 = 2x$, $y_2 = 2$.

$$R = \frac{(1+4x^2)^{3/2}}{2}$$

At origin: $R = 1/2$. At $(1,1)$: $R = (1+4)^{3/2}/2 = 5\sqrt{5}/2 \approx 5.59$.

Example 2 — $y = \sin x$

$y_1 = \cos x$, $y_2 = -\sin x$.

$$R = \frac{(1+\cos^2 x)^{3/2}}{|\sin x|}$$

At $x = \pi/2$: $R = (1+0)^{3/2}/1 = 1$. As $x\to 0$, $R \to \infty$ (inflection point).

Example 3 — Rectangular Hyperbola $xy = c^2$

$y = c^2/x$, $y_1 = -c^2/x^2$, $y_2 = 2c^2/x^3$.

$$R = \frac{\left(1+\frac{c^4}{x^4}\right)^{3/2}}{\left|\frac{2c^2}{x^3}\right|} = \frac{(x^4+c^4)^{3/2}}{2c^2 x^3}$$

At the point $(c,c)$: $R = (c^4+c^4)^{3/2}/(2c^2\cdot c^3) = (2c^4)^{3/2}/(2c^5) = 2^{3/2}c^6/(2c^5) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot 2c = c\sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{2}{2} = c\sqrt{2}$.

More carefully: $(x^4+c^4)^{3/2}\big|_{x=c} = (2c^4)^{3/2} = 2^{3/2}c^6$; denominator $= 2c^2\cdot c^3 = 2c^5$. So $R = 2^{3/2}c^6/(2c^5) = 2^{1/2}c = c\sqrt{2}$.

Example 4 — Ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$

At a general point $(a\cos\theta, b\sin\theta)$, using $\kappa$ from Section 2:

$$R = \frac{(a^2\sin^2\theta + b^2\cos^2\theta)^{3/2}}{ab}$$

At end of major axis $(\pm a, 0)$: $\theta = 0$, $R = b^3/(ab) = b^2/a$.

At end of minor axis $(0,\pm b)$: $\theta = \pi/2$, $R = a^3/(ab) = a^2/b$.

When $a > b$, the ellipse has smaller $R$ (sharper curvature) at the ends of the major axis.

4

Radius of Curvature — Polar Form

4.1 Derivation

For $r = f(\theta)$, let $r_1 = dr/d\theta$, $r_2 = d^2r/d\theta^2$. Using the parametric curvature formula with $\theta$ as parameter:

$$x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta$$ $$\dot x = r_1\cos\theta - r\sin\theta,\quad \dot y = r_1\sin\theta + r\cos\theta$$ $$\dot x^2 + \dot y^2 = r^2 + r_1^2$$ $$\ddot x = r_2\cos\theta - 2r_1\sin\theta - r\cos\theta,\quad \ddot y = r_2\sin\theta + 2r_1\cos\theta - r\sin\theta$$ $$\dot x\ddot y - \dot y\ddot x = r^2 + 2r_1^2 - rr_2$$

Radius of Curvature — Polar Form

$$R = \frac{(r^2+r_1^2)^{3/2}}{|r^2 + 2r_1^2 - rr_2|}$$

4.2 Pedal Form

The pedal length $p$ (perpendicular from pole $O$ to the tangent at $P$) satisfies:

$$\frac{1}{p^2} = \frac{1}{r^2} + \frac{1}{r^4}\left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2 \implies \frac{1}{p^2} = \frac{r^2+r_1^2}{r^4}$$ $$p = \frac{r^2}{\sqrt{r^2+r_1^2}}$$

Pedal Form of Radius of Curvature

$$R = r\frac{dr}{dp}$$

This elegant relation means: given the pedal equation $p = g(r)$, differentiate to get $dp/dr$, then $R = r/(dp/dr)$.

Example 1 — Archimedean Spiral: $r = a\theta$

$r_1 = a$, $r_2 = 0$.

$$R = \frac{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}}{|r^2 + 2a^2 - 0|} = \frac{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}}{r^2+2a^2}$$

At $\theta = 0$ (pole): $R = a^3/(2a^2) = a/2$ (finite — the spiral has nonzero curvature at the pole).

Example 2 — Cardioid: $r = a(1+\cos\theta)$

$r_1 = -a\sin\theta$, $r_2 = -a\cos\theta$.

$$r^2 + r_1^2 = a^2(1+\cos\theta)^2 + a^2\sin^2\theta = a^2(2+2\cos\theta) = 2a^2(1+\cos\theta)$$ $$r^2+2r_1^2 - rr_2 = a^2(1+\cos\theta)^2 + 2a^2\sin^2\theta + a^2(1+\cos\theta)\cos\theta$$

After simplification using $1+\cos\theta = 2\cos^2(\theta/2)$:

$$r^2+2r_1^2-rr_2 = \tfrac{3}{2}a^2(1+\cos\theta) \cdot 2 = 3a^2(1+\cos\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^2+2r_1^2-rr_2 = \frac{3a^2(1+\cos\theta)+a^2\cos\theta\cdot a}{?}$$

Using $2\cos^2(\theta/2) = 1+\cos\theta$:

$$r^2+r_1^2 = 4a^2\cos^2(\theta/2), \quad r^2+2r_1^2-rr_2 = 3\cdot 2a^2\cos^2(\theta/2)\cdot \frac{3}{2} = \frac{3}{2}\cdot 2a^2(1+\cos\theta)$$

Actually the clean result is:

$$R = \frac{[2a^2(1+\cos\theta)]^{3/2}}{3a^2(1+\cos\theta)} = \frac{2^{3/2}a^3(1+\cos\theta)^{3/2}}{3a^2(1+\cos\theta)} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}\,a\sqrt{1+\cos\theta}}{3}$$

Using $\sqrt{1+\cos\theta} = \sqrt{2}\cos(\theta/2)$:

$$\boxed{R = \frac{2}{3}\sqrt{2ar}}$$

where $r = a(1+\cos\theta)$. So $R^2 = 8ar/9$ — the radius of curvature of the cardioid at the point $(r,\theta)$.

Example 3 — Lemniscate $r^2 = a^2\cos 2\theta$

Differentiate: $2r r_1 = -2a^2\sin 2\theta$, so $r r_1 = -a^2\sin 2\theta$.

$r^2 + r_1^2 = a^2\cos 2\theta + \frac{a^4\sin^2 2\theta}{r^2} = a^2\cos 2\theta + \frac{a^4\sin^2 2\theta}{a^2\cos 2\theta} = \frac{a^4}{r^2}$.

After careful computation: $R = a^2/(3r)$, or equivalently $3Rr = a^2$.

5

Radius of Curvature — Parametric Form

5.1 Derivation

For $x = x(t)$, $y = y(t)$ (dots = $d/dt$):

$$y_1 = \frac{\dot y}{\dot x}, \qquad y_2 = \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\dot y}{\dot x}\right)\!\cdot\!\frac{1}{\dot x} = \frac{\dot x\ddot y - \dot y\ddot x}{\dot x^3}$$ $$1+y_1^2 = 1 + \frac{\dot y^2}{\dot x^2} = \frac{\dot x^2+\dot y^2}{\dot x^2}$$ $$(1+y_1^2)^{3/2} = \frac{(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)^{3/2}}{|\dot x|^3}$$

Radius of Curvature — Parametric Form

$$R = \frac{(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2)^{3/2}}{|\dot x\ddot y - \dot y\ddot x|}$$

Example 1 — Cycloid: $x = a(t-\sin t)$, $y = a(1-\cos t)$

$$\dot x = a(1-\cos t),\quad \dot y = a\sin t$$ $$\ddot x = a\sin t,\quad \ddot y = a\cos t$$ $$\dot x^2+\dot y^2 = a^2[(1-\cos t)^2+\sin^2 t] = a^2[2-2\cos t] = 4a^2\sin^2(t/2)$$ $$\dot x\ddot y - \dot y\ddot x = a(1-\cos t)\cdot a\cos t - a\sin t\cdot a\sin t = a^2(\cos t - \cos^2 t - \sin^2 t) = a^2(\cos t - 1) = -a^2(1-\cos t)$$ $$R = \frac{[4a^2\sin^2(t/2)]^{3/2}}{a^2(1-\cos t)} = \frac{8a^3\sin^3(t/2)}{2a^2\sin^2(t/2)} = 4a\sin(t/2)$$ $$\boxed{R_{\text{cycloid}} = 4a\sin(t/2)}$$

At $t=\pi$ (apex): $R = 4a\sin(\pi/2) = 4a$. At cusps ($t=0,2\pi$): $R = 0$. The cusp is infinitely sharp.

Example 2 — Ellipse: $x = a\cos t$, $y = b\sin t$

$$\dot x = -a\sin t,\quad \dot y = b\cos t,\quad \ddot x = -a\cos t,\quad \ddot y = -b\sin t$$ $$\dot x\ddot y - \dot y\ddot x = (-a\sin t)(-b\sin t)-(b\cos t)(-a\cos t) = ab$$ $$R = \frac{(a^2\sin^2 t + b^2\cos^2 t)^{3/2}}{ab}$$

Confirms our earlier calculation.

Example 3 — Astroid: $x = a\cos^3 t$, $y = a\sin^3 t$

$$\dot x = -3a\cos^2 t\sin t,\quad \dot y = 3a\sin^2 t\cos t$$ $$\dot x^2+\dot y^2 = 9a^2\cos^2 t\sin^2 t(\cos^2 t+\sin^2 t) = 9a^2\sin^2 t\cos^2 t$$ $$\ddot x = -3a(2\cos t(-\sin t)\sin t + \cos^2 t\cos t) = 3a\cos t(2\sin^2 t - \cos^2 t)$$ $$\ddot y = 3a\sin t(2\cos^2 t - \sin^2 t)$$ $$\dot x\ddot y - \dot y\ddot x = -3a\cos^2 t\sin t\cdot 3a\sin t(2\cos^2 t-\sin^2 t) - 3a\sin^2 t\cos t\cdot 3a\cos t(2\sin^2 t-\cos^2 t)$$

Simplifying: $= -9a^2\sin t\cos t$. So:

$$R = \frac{(9a^2\sin^2 t\cos^2 t)^{3/2}}{9a^2|\sin t\cos t|} = \frac{27a^3|\sin t\cos t|^3}{9a^2|\sin t\cos t|} = 3a|\sin t\cos t| = \frac{3a}{2}|\sin 2t|$$

At the four cusps ($t = 0, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2$): $R = 0$. Max $R = 3a/2$ at $t = \pi/4, 3\pi/4$.

Circle of Curvature

Osculating Circle

At a point $P$ on a curve, the circle of curvature (osculating circle) is the unique circle that:

  • passes through $P$,
  • has the same tangent as the curve at $P$,
  • has the same curvature $\kappa$ as the curve at $P$.

Its radius is $R = 1/\kappa$ and its centre $C(\alpha, \beta)$ lies on the normal to the curve at $P$.

6.1 Centre of Curvature

If the curve has point $P = (x, y)$ with slope $y_1$ and second derivative $y_2$, the centre $(\alpha, \beta)$ lies at distance $R$ along the inward normal:

Centre of Curvature Formulas

$$\alpha = x - \frac{y_1(1+y_1^2)}{y_2}, \qquad \beta = y + \frac{1+y_1^2}{y_2}$$

Derivation

The normal at $(x,y)$ has direction vector $(y_1, -1)$ (rotated tangent). The unit normal pointing toward the centre of curvature (inward when $y_2 > 0$) is $\hat n = \frac{(y_1, -1)}{\sqrt{1+y_1^2}}$ (or the opposite sign for $y_2 < 0$, adjusting automatically). Then:

$$(\alpha-x, \beta-y) = \pm R\cdot\hat n = \frac{-(1+y_1^2)}{y_2}(y_1, -1)$$ $$\alpha = x - \frac{y_1(1+y_1^2)}{y_2}, \qquad \beta = y + \frac{1+y_1^2}{y_2}$$

Example 1 — Circle of Curvature for $y = x^2$ at $(0,0)$

$y_1(0) = 0$, $y_2(0) = 2$.

$$\alpha = 0 - \frac{0 \cdot 1}{2} = 0, \qquad \beta = 0 + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad R = \frac{1}{2}$$

Circle: $x^2 + (y - \tfrac12)^2 = \tfrac14$. This is a circle of radius $\tfrac12$ centred at $(0,\tfrac12)$.

Example 2 — Circle of Curvature for $y = x^2$ at $(1,1)$

$y_1(1) = 2$, $y_2(1) = 2$.

$$\alpha = 1 - \frac{2(1+4)}{2} = 1 - 5 = -4, \qquad \beta = 1 + \frac{1+4}{2} = 1 + \frac{5}{2} = \frac{7}{2}$$ $$R = \frac{(1+4)^{3/2}}{2} = \frac{5\sqrt{5}}{2}$$

Circle of curvature: $(x+4)^2 + (y - 7/2)^2 = 125/4$.

Example 3 — Circle of Curvature for $y = \ln x$ at $(1,0)$

$y_1 = 1/x$, $y_2 = -1/x^2$. At $x=1$: $y_1 = 1$, $y_2 = -1$.

$$\alpha = 1 - \frac{1\cdot(1+1)}{-1} = 1 + 2 = 3, \qquad \beta = 0 + \frac{1+1}{-1} = -2$$ $$R = \frac{(1+1)^{3/2}}{1} = 2\sqrt{2}$$

Circle: $(x-3)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 8$.

6.2 Evolute as Locus of Centres

Evolute Definition via Osculating Circle

As $P$ moves along the curve $C$, the centre of curvature $(\alpha(t), \beta(t))$ traces a new curve called the evolute of $C$. The original curve $C$ is an involute of its evolute.

7

Evolutes and Involutes

Evolute

The evolute of a curve $C$ is the locus of its centres of curvature. If $C$ is given parametrically, its evolute is obtained by eliminating the parameter from:

$$\alpha = x - \frac{y_1(1+y_1^2)}{y_2}, \qquad \beta = y + \frac{1+y_1^2}{y_2}$$

Involute

An involute of a curve $E$ is a curve $C$ whose evolute is $E$. Geometrically, the involute is traced by the free end of a taut string as it is unwound from the curve $E$.

Key Properties

  • Every tangent to the evolute is normal to the involute.
  • The evolute of an involute is the original curve.
  • The difference of arc lengths on the evolute between two points equals the difference of radii of curvature at the corresponding points of the involute.

7.1 Evolute of the Parabola $y^2 = 4ax$

Parametrise: $x = at^2$, $y = 2at$. Then $\dot x = 2at$, $\dot y = 2a$, $\ddot x = 2a$, $\ddot y = 0$.

$$y_1 = \frac{2a}{2at} = \frac{1}{t}, \qquad y_2 = \frac{\dot x\ddot y - \dot y\ddot x}{\dot x^3} = \frac{0 - 2a\cdot 2a}{(2at)^3} = \frac{-4a^2}{8a^3t^3} = \frac{-1}{2at^3}$$ $$1+y_1^2 = 1+\frac{1}{t^2} = \frac{t^2+1}{t^2}$$ $$\alpha = at^2 - \frac{\frac{1}{t}\cdot\frac{t^2+1}{t^2}}{\frac{-1}{2at^3}} = at^2 + 2a(t^2+1) = at^2 + 2at^2 + 2a = 3at^2 + 2a$$ $$\beta = 2at + \frac{\frac{t^2+1}{t^2}}{\frac{-1}{2at^3}} = 2at - 2at(t^2+1) = 2at - 2at^3 - 2at = -2at^3$$

So $\alpha = 3at^2 + 2a$ and $\beta = -2at^3$. To eliminate $t$:

$$t^2 = \frac{\alpha - 2a}{3a}, \qquad t^3 = -\frac{\beta}{2a}$$ $$t^6 = \left(\frac{\alpha-2a}{3a}\right)^3 = \frac{\beta^2}{4a^2}$$ $$\frac{(\alpha-2a)^3}{27a^3} = \frac{\beta^2}{4a^2}$$

Evolute of Parabola $y^2 = 4ax$

$$27a\beta^2 = 4(\alpha-2a)^3 \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad 27ay^2 = 4(x-2a)^3$$

This is itself a semi-cubical parabola with vertex at $(2a, 0)$.

7.2 Evolute of the Ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$

Using $x = a\cos t$, $y = b\sin t$:

$$y_1 = -\frac{b\cos t}{a\sin t}, \qquad y_2 = \frac{\dot x\ddot y - \dot y\ddot x}{\dot x^3} = \frac{(-a\sin t)(-b\sin t) - (b\cos t)(-a\cos t)}{-a^3\sin^3 t} = \frac{ab}{-a^3\sin^3 t} = \frac{-b}{a^2\sin^3 t}$$ $$\alpha = a\cos t - \frac{(-\frac{b\cos t}{a\sin t})(1+\frac{b^2\cos^2 t}{a^2\sin^2 t})}{\frac{-b}{a^2\sin^3 t}} = a\cos t - \frac{a^2-b^2}{a}\cos^3 t = \frac{a^2-b^2}{a}\cos^3 t + a\cos t - \frac{a^2-b^2}{a}\cos^3 t$$

Carefully:

$$\alpha = a\cos t - \frac{b\cos t}{a\sin t}\cdot\frac{a^2\sin^3 t}{b}\cdot\frac{1}{\sin^2 t}\cdot(-1)\cdot(-1)\cdot\frac{a^2\sin^2 t+b^2\cos^2 t}{a^2\sin^2 t}$$

The standard result is:

$$\alpha = \frac{a^2-b^2}{a}\cos^3 t, \qquad \beta = -\frac{a^2-b^2}{b}\sin^3 t$$

Let $c^2 = a^2-b^2$. Then $\cos^3 t = a\alpha/c^2$, $\sin^3 t = -b\beta/c^2$. Using $\cos^{2/3} t + \sin^{2/3} t = 1$... actually $\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$ gives:

$$(a\alpha/c^2)^{2/3} + (b\beta/c^2)^{2/3} = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$$

Evolute of Ellipse (Astroid)

$$(a\alpha)^{2/3} + (b\beta)^{2/3} = (a^2-b^2)^{2/3}$$

This is an astroid (four-cusped hypocycloid).

7.3 Involute — String Interpretation

Unwinding String

Wrap a string tightly around a curve $E$ (the evolute). Attach a pen to the free end and unwind the string, keeping it taut. The pen traces an involute of $E$.

Different starting points on the string give parallel involutes (a family of involutes, all with the same evolute).

Classic application: The involute of a circle is the tooth profile used in most modern gear systems (involute gears), ensuring a constant velocity ratio as gears mesh.

Example — Involute of a Circle of Radius $a$

Let the circle be $x = a\cos t$, $y = a\sin t$. Arc length from $t=0$: $s = at$. The unit tangent is $(\sin t, -\cos t)$ (outward). The involute:

$$X = a\cos t + at\sin t, \qquad Y = a\sin t - at\cos t$$

This is used as the standard involute gear profile.

8

Envelopes

Envelope of a Family of Curves

Given a one-parameter family of curves $F(x, y, c) = 0$, the envelope is a curve that is tangent to every member of the family. At each point of tangency, the envelope and the curve $F = 0$ (for the corresponding $c$) share both a common point and a common tangent.

8.1 Method of Finding the Envelope

Elimination Method

To find the envelope of $F(x,y,c) = 0$:

  1. Differentiate with respect to $c$ (treating $x,y$ as constants): $\frac{\partial F}{\partial c} = 0$.
  2. Eliminate $c$ between $F = 0$ and $\partial F/\partial c = 0$.
  3. The resulting equation in $x$ and $y$ is the envelope (plus possibly singular points of the family).

Discriminant Method

If $F(x,y,c) = 0$ can be written as a polynomial in $c$, the envelope is obtained by setting the discriminant of this polynomial (with respect to $c$) to zero.

For a quadratic $Ac^2 + Bc + C = 0$: discriminant $= B^2 - 4AC = 0$ is the envelope.

Example 1 — Family of Lines: $\frac{x}{c} + \frac{y}{k-c} = 1$ (constant intercept sum $k$)

$F = \frac{x}{c} + \frac{y}{k-c} - 1 = 0$.

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial c} = -\frac{x}{c^2} + \frac{y}{(k-c)^2} = 0 \implies \frac{y}{(k-c)^2} = \frac{x}{c^2} \implies \frac{\sqrt{y}}{k-c} = \frac{\sqrt{x}}{c}$$ $$c = \frac{k\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}}, \quad k-c = \frac{k\sqrt{y}}{\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}}$$

Substituting back into $F = 0$:

$$\frac{\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}}{k\sqrt{x}}\cdot x + \frac{\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}}{k\sqrt{y}}\cdot y = 1$$ $$\frac{\sqrt{x}(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}) + \sqrt{y}(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y})}{k\sqrt{xy}} = 1 \implies \frac{(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y})^2}{k\sqrt{xy}} = 1$$ $$\boxed{\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{y} = \sqrt{k}}$$

The envelope is a parabola (in the $\sqrt{x}, \sqrt{y}$ variables).

Example 2 — Family of Circles: $(x-c)^2 + y^2 = r^2$ (centres on $x$-axis)

$F = (x-c)^2 + y^2 - r^2 = 0$.

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial c} = -2(x-c) = 0 \implies c = x$$

Substituting: $0 + y^2 = r^2 \implies y = \pm r$. The envelope consists of the two horizontal lines $y = r$ and $y = -r$ — the lines tangent to all circles from above and below.

Example 3 — Discriminant: $y = cx - c^2$ (family of tangents to a parabola)

Rewrite: $c^2 - cx + y = 0$ (quadratic in $c$). Discriminant $= x^2 - 4y = 0$.

$$\boxed{y = \frac{x^2}{4}}$$

The envelope is the parabola $x^2 = 4y$, which is tangent to every line $y = cx - c^2$ (these are precisely the tangent lines to $x^2 = 4y$).

Example 4 — Family of Ellipses: $\frac{x^2}{c^2}+\frac{y^2}{(1-c)^2}=1$, $0<c<1$

$F = \frac{x^2}{c^2} + \frac{y^2}{(1-c)^2} - 1 = 0$.

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial c} = -\frac{2x^2}{c^3} + \frac{2y^2}{(1-c)^3} = 0 \implies \frac{x^2}{c^3} = \frac{y^2}{(1-c)^3}$$ $$\left(\frac{c}{1-c}\right)^3 = \frac{x^2}{y^2} \implies \frac{c}{1-c} = \left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{2/3} \implies c = \frac{x^{2/3}}{x^{2/3}+y^{2/3}}$$

Substituting into $F = 0$ gives:

$$\boxed{x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = 1}$$

The envelope is an astroid — a beautiful result connecting ellipses to the four-cusped curve.

8.2 Applications — Caustics

Caustics in Optics

When parallel light rays reflect off a curved mirror, the reflected rays form a family of lines whose envelope is the caustic curve — the bright curve you see at the bottom of a coffee cup in sunlight. The caustic of a circular mirror for parallel rays is a cardioid; for a parabolic mirror (by design) it degenerates to a single point — the focus.

9

Pedal Equations

Pedal Equation Definition

The pedal equation of a curve with respect to a fixed pole $O$ (usually the origin or the focus) is a relation between:

  • $r$ = distance from the pole $O$ to the point $P$ on the curve, and
  • $p$ = length of the perpendicular dropped from $O$ to the tangent at $P$.

It expresses the intrinsic geometry of the curve relative to the pole, independent of orientation.

9.1 Key Formula

For a polar curve $r = f(\theta)$, the perpendicular length from the pole to the tangent is:

Pedal Length

$$\frac{1}{p^2} = \frac{1}{r^2} + \frac{1}{r^4}\left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p = \frac{r^2}{\sqrt{r^2 + r_1^2}}$$

Derivation: If $\phi$ is the angle between the radius vector and the tangent, then $p = r\sin\phi$ and $\tan\phi = r/(dr/d\theta) = r/r_1$. So $\sin\phi = r/\sqrt{r^2+r_1^2}$, giving $p = r^2/\sqrt{r^2+r_1^2}$.

9.2 Pedal Equation from Cartesian Form

For a curve $F(x,y)=0$ with pole at origin, the tangent at $(x_0,y_0)$ has equation:

$$F_x(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0) + F_y(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0) = 0$$ $$p = \frac{|x_0 F_x + y_0 F_y - (x_0 F_x + y_0 F_y)|}{\sqrt{F_x^2+F_y^2}} = \frac{|x_0 F_y - y_0 F_x| \cdot 0}{\ldots}$$

More directly, $p$ = distance from origin to tangent line. Given tangent $ax + by = c$, $p = |c|/\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.

Example 1 — Pedal Equation of the Parabola $r = \ell/(1-\cos\theta)$ (focus as pole)

Semi-latus rectum $\ell = 2a$. Then $r_1 = -\ell\sin\theta/(1-\cos\theta)^2 = -r\sin\theta/(1-\cos\theta)$.

$$r^2 + r_1^2 = r^2\left[1 + \frac{\sin^2\theta}{(1-\cos\theta)^2}\right] = r^2\cdot\frac{(1-\cos\theta)^2 + \sin^2\theta}{(1-\cos\theta)^2} = r^2\cdot\frac{2(1-\cos\theta)}{(1-\cos\theta)^2} = \frac{2r^2}{r/\ell} \cdot \frac{1}{r}$$

Noting $1-\cos\theta = \ell/r$: $r^2+r_1^2 = r^2\cdot\frac{2\ell/r}{(\ell/r)^2} = \frac{2r^3}{\ell}$.

$$p = \frac{r^2}{\sqrt{2r^3/\ell}} = r^2\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{2r^3}} = \sqrt{\frac{\ell r}{2}}$$ $$\boxed{p^2 = \frac{\ell r}{2} = ar} \quad (\ell = 2a)$$

This is the pedal equation of a parabola with focus as pole: $p^2 = ar$.

Example 2 — Pedal Equation of the Ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$ (centre as pole)

The perpendicular from the centre to the tangent at $(a\cos t, b\sin t)$ is $p$, where the tangent is $\frac{x\cos t}{a} + \frac{y\sin t}{b} = 1$:

$$p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\cos^2 t/a^2 + \sin^2 t/b^2}}$$ $$\frac{1}{p^2} = \frac{\cos^2 t}{a^2} + \frac{\sin^2 t}{b^2}$$

Also $r^2 = a^2\cos^2 t + b^2\sin^2 t$. To eliminate $t$:

$$\frac{1}{p^2} = \frac{\cos^2 t}{a^2} + \frac{\sin^2 t}{b^2} = \frac{b^2\cos^2 t + a^2\sin^2 t}{a^2 b^2}$$ $$r^2 = a^2\cos^2 t + b^2\sin^2 t$$

We find $r^2 + a^2b^2/p^2 = a^2\cos^2 t + b^2\sin^2 t + a^2\sin^2 t + b^2\cos^2 t = a^2+b^2$. Therefore:

$$\boxed{\frac{a^2b^2}{p^2} = a^2+b^2 - r^2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{p^2} = \frac{a^2+b^2-r^2}{a^2 b^2}$$

Example 3 — Pedal Equation of Cardioid $r = a(1+\cos\theta)$ (pole at cusp)

$r_1 = -a\sin\theta$. From Example 2 in Section 4:

$$r^2 + r_1^2 = 2a^2(1+\cos\theta) = 2ar$$ $$p = \frac{r^2}{\sqrt{2ar}} = \frac{r^2}{\sqrt{2ar}} = r\sqrt{\frac{r}{2a}}$$ $$\boxed{p^2 = \frac{r^3}{2a}}$$

Equivalently, $2ap^2 = r^3$ — the pedal equation of the cardioid.

Example 4 — Pedal Equation of Lemniscate $r^2 = a^2\cos 2\theta$

From Section 4: $r^2 + r_1^2 = a^4/r^2$. So:

$$p = \frac{r^2}{\sqrt{a^4/r^2}} = \frac{r^2}{a^2/r} = \frac{r^3}{a^2}$$ $$\boxed{p = \frac{r^3}{a^2}} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a^2 p = r^3$$

Note: from the pedal form $R = r\,dr/dp = r\cdot dr/(3r^2/a^2\,dr) = a^2/(3r)$, confirming $R = a^2/(3r)$.

9.3 Bertrand Curves

Bertrand Curves

Two space curves $C_1$ and $C_2$ are called Bertrand mates if they have the same principal normals at corresponding points. Equivalently, a Bertrand curve satisfies a linear relation between its curvature $\kappa$ and torsion $\tau$:

$$a\kappa + b\tau = 1, \quad a,b \text{ constants}$$

Special case: if $b = 0$, then $\kappa = \text{const}$ — a circular helix.

In the plane (2D context), every curve is a Bertrand curve with its parallel curves as mates.
