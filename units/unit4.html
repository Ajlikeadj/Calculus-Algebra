Section 01

Review of Limits, Continuity & Differentiability

Extending the notion of limits and continuity from one variable to two (or more) variables introduces fundamentally new complexity. In one variable, a point can be approached from only two directions; in two variables, there are infinitely many approach paths, making limit calculations and existence proofs far more subtle.

Definition of a Limit

Limit of $f(x,y)$: We say $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y) = L$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that

$$0 < \sqrt{(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2} < \delta \implies |f(x,y) - L| < \varepsilon$$

That is, $f(x,y)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $L$ whenever $(x,y)$ is sufficiently close (but not equal) to $(a,b)$ — regardless of the direction of approach.

The key distinction from single-variable calculus is the phrase "regardless of the direction of approach." In single-variable calculus, only the left and right limits exist. In two variables, $(x,y)$ can approach $(a,b)$ along any curve, so the limit must be the same along all paths simultaneously.

Open Disk (Neighborhood): The open disk of radius $\delta$ centred at $(a,b)$ is the set

$$D_\delta(a,b) = \{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : \sqrt{(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2} < \delta\}$$

The punctured disk excludes the centre point itself. The limit definition requires $f$ to be defined on some punctured neighbourhood of $(a,b)$.

Example 1.1 — Basic Limit

Find $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(1,2)}\left(3x^2 + xy - y^2\right)$.

Solution: Since $f(x,y) = 3x^2 + xy - y^2$ is a polynomial, it is continuous everywhere. Therefore we simply substitute:

$$\lim_{(x,y)\to(1,2)} (3x^2 + xy - y^2) = 3(1)^2 + (1)(2) - (2)^2 = 3 + 2 - 4 = \boxed{1}$$

Path-Dependence Test for Non-Existence

If $f(x,y)$ approaches different values along two different paths toward $(a,b)$, then $\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y)$ does not exist. This is the most powerful tool for disproving limits.

Example 1.2 — Limit Does Not Exist

Show that $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \frac{x^2 - y^2}{x^2 + y^2}$ does not exist.

  • Path 1: along $y=0$.
    $f(x,0) = \dfrac{x^2 - 0}{x^2 + 0} = 1 \to 1$ as $x\to 0$.
  • Path 2: along $x=0$.
    $f(0,y) = \dfrac{0 - y^2}{0 + y^2} = -1 \to -1$ as $y\to 0$.
  • Since the two paths give different limits ($1 \neq -1$), the limit does not exist. $\blacksquare$
Example 1.3 — A Subtler Non-Existence

Investigate $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}$.

  • Along $y = mx$: $f(x,mx) = \dfrac{x\cdot mx}{x^2+m^2x^2} = \dfrac{mx^2}{(1+m^2)x^2} = \dfrac{m}{1+m^2}$, which depends on $m$.
  • Since different straight-line paths give different limits, the limit does not exist.
Example 1.4 — A Limit That Does Exist (Squeeze Theorem)

Show $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^2 y}{x^2+y^2} = 0$.

  • Note $|y| \leq \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, so $\left|\dfrac{x^2 y}{x^2+y^2}\right| \leq \dfrac{x^2\,|y|}{x^2+y^2} \leq \dfrac{(x^2+y^2)|y|}{x^2+y^2} = |y|$.
  • Also $\left|\dfrac{x^2 y}{x^2+y^2}\right| \leq |y| \to 0$ as $(x,y)\to(0,0)$.
  • By the Squeeze Theorem, the limit equals $0$. $\blacksquare$

Continuity of Functions of Two Variables

A function $f(x,y)$ is continuous at $(a,b)$ if:

  • $f(a,b)$ is defined,
  • $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y)$ exists,
  • $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y) = f(a,b)$.
Example 1.5 — Discontinuous at Origin

The function $f(x,y) = \begin{cases}\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2} & (x,y)\neq(0,0)\\ 0 & (x,y)=(0,0)\end{cases}$ is discontinuous at the origin because the limit does not exist (shown in Ex 1.3), yet $f(0,0)=0$ is defined.

Iterated Limits vs Simultaneous Limits

The iterated limits are computed by applying one variable at a time:

$$\lim_{x\to a}\left[\lim_{y\to b} f(x,y)\right] \quad\text{and}\quad \lim_{y\to b}\left[\lim_{x\to a} f(x,y)\right]$$
Even when both iterated limits exist and are equal, the simultaneous limit $\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)$ need not exist. Equality of iterated limits is necessary but not sufficient for the simultaneous limit to exist.
Example 1.6 — Iterated ≠ Simultaneous

Let $f(x,y) = \dfrac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2}$.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\left[\lim_{y\to 0}\frac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2}\right] = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x^2}{x^2} = 1$

$\displaystyle\lim_{y\to 0}\left[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2}\right] = \lim_{y\to 0}\frac{-y^2}{y^2} = -1$

Both iterated limits exist but differ — confirming the simultaneous limit does not exist.

Key Takeaways — Limits & Continuity
  • The simultaneous limit requires agreement along all paths, not just straight lines.
  • To show a limit doesn't exist: find two paths giving different values.
  • To show a limit does exist: use the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition or Squeeze Theorem.
  • Polynomials and rational functions (away from zeros of denominator) are continuous.
  • Compositions of continuous functions are continuous.
Section 02

Introduction to Partial Derivatives

A partial derivative measures the rate of change of a multivariable function with respect to one variable while all other variables are held constant. It is the fundamental building block of multivariable differential calculus.

The partial derivative of $f(x,y)$ with respect to $x$ at the point $(a,b)$ is

$$f_x(a,b) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\bigg|_{(a,b)} = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h,b) - f(a,b)}{h}$$

Similarly, the partial derivative with respect to $y$ is

$$f_y(a,b) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\bigg|_{(a,b)} = \lim_{k\to 0}\frac{f(a,b+k) - f(a,b)}{k}$$

Notations: $\partial f/\partial x$, $f_x$, $D_x f$, $\partial_x f$ are all equivalent.

To compute $\partial f/\partial x$: treat $y$ as a constant and differentiate with respect to $x$ using the ordinary rules of single-variable calculus.

Worked Examples — First Partial Derivatives

Example 2.1

Find $f_x$ and $f_y$ for $f(x,y) = x^3y^2 - 4x^2y + 7xy^3$.

$$f_x = 3x^2 y^2 - 8xy + 7y^3, \qquad f_y = 2x^3 y - 4x^2 + 21xy^2$$
Example 2.2

Find all first partial derivatives of $f(x,y) = e^{xy}\sin(x+y)$.

  • $f_x = ye^{xy}\sin(x+y) + e^{xy}\cos(x+y) = e^{xy}[y\sin(x+y)+\cos(x+y)]$
  • $f_y = xe^{xy}\sin(x+y) + e^{xy}\cos(x+y) = e^{xy}[x\sin(x+y)+\cos(x+y)]$
Example 2.3

Find $\partial f/\partial x$ and $\partial f/\partial y$ for $f(x,y) = \ln(x^2+y^2)$ (for $(x,y)\neq(0,0)$).

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \frac{2x}{x^2+y^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{2y}{x^2+y^2}$$
Example 2.4

For $f(x,y,z) = x^2 yz + \cos(xz) - e^{yz}$, find all three first partials.

$$f_x = 2xyz - z\sin(xz)$$ $$f_y = x^2 z - ze^{yz}$$ $$f_z = x^2 y - x\sin(xz) - ye^{yz}$$
Example 2.5

Find $\partial f/\partial x$ for $f(x,y) = \tan^{-1}(y/x)$.

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \frac{1}{1+(y/x)^2}\cdot\left(-\frac{y}{x^2}\right) = \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}$$

Higher-Order Partial Derivatives

We may differentiate again to obtain second-order partial derivatives. There are four of them for a function of two variables:

$$f_{xx} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}, \quad f_{xy} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}, \quad f_{yx} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}, \quad f_{yy} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}$$

Note the notational convention: $f_{xy}$ means "differentiate first with respect to $x$, then with respect to $y$." The notation $\partial^2 f/\partial y\,\partial x$ means the same — differentiate first (inner) with respect to $x$, then (outer) with respect to $y$.

Let $f$ be defined on an open set $D \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ containing $(a,b)$. If $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ are both continuous on $D$, then

$$f_{xy}(a,b) = f_{yx}(a,b)$$

That is, the order of mixed partial differentiation may be interchanged whenever the mixed partials are continuous.

Clairaut's theorem fails when the mixed partials are discontinuous. The classic counterexample: $f(x,y) = xy(x^2-y^2)/(x^2+y^2)$ for $(x,y)\neq(0,0)$, $f(0,0)=0$ — here $f_{xy}(0,0)\neq f_{yx}(0,0)$.
Example 2.6 — All Second Partials

Find all second partial derivatives of $f(x,y) = x^3y - 2x^2y^3 + 5y$.

  • $f_x = 3x^2y - 4xy^3$, $\quad f_y = x^3 - 6x^2y^2 + 5$
  • $f_{xx} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(3x^2y - 4xy^3) = 6xy - 4y^3$
  • $f_{yy} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^3 - 6x^2y^2 + 5) = -12x^2 y$
  • $f_{xy} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}(3x^2y - 4xy^3) = 3x^2 - 12xy^2$
  • $f_{yx} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^3 - 6x^2y^2 + 5) = 3x^2 - 12xy^2 = f_{xy}$ ✓ (Clairaut's verified)
Example 2.7 — Partial Derivatives of Implicit Function

If $z$ is defined implicitly by $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = a^2$, find $\partial z/\partial x$ and $\partial z/\partial y$.

  • Differentiate both sides w.r.t. $x$, treating $y$ as constant and $z$ as function of $x$: $2x + 2z\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x} = 0$
  • $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\dfrac{x}{z}$
  • Similarly: $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y} = -\dfrac{y}{z}$
Section 03

Geometric Interpretation of Partial Derivatives

Partial derivatives have beautiful geometric meanings: they represent slopes of cross-sectional curves, define the tangent plane to a surface, and generate the gradient vector that points in the direction of steepest ascent.

Tangent Plane to a Surface

Geometrically, $\partial f/\partial x$ at $(a,b)$ is the slope of the curve formed by intersecting the surface $z=f(x,y)$ with the plane $y=b$. Similarly, $\partial f/\partial y$ is the slope of the cross-section in the plane $x=a$.

The equation of the tangent plane to the surface $z = f(x,y)$ at the point $P_0 = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$ where $z_0 = f(x_0,y_0)$ is:

$$z - z_0 = f_x(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0) + f_y(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0)$$

The normal line to the surface at $P_0$ has parametric equations:

$$\frac{x-x_0}{f_x(x_0,y_0)} = \frac{y-y_0}{f_y(x_0,y_0)} = \frac{z-z_0}{-1}$$
Example 3.1 — Tangent Plane

Find the tangent plane to $z = x^2 + y^2$ at $(1, 2, 5)$.

  • $f_x = 2x$, so $f_x(1,2) = 2$. $\quad f_y = 2y$, so $f_y(1,2) = 4$.
  • Tangent plane: $z - 5 = 2(x-1) + 4(y-2)$
  • Simplified: $z = 2x + 4y - 5$ or $2x + 4y - z = 5$. $\blacksquare$
Example 3.2 — Tangent Plane and Normal Line

Find the tangent plane and normal line to $z = x^2 - 2xy + y^3$ at $(1,1,0)$.

  • $f_x = 2x - 2y$; at $(1,1)$: $f_x = 0$. $\quad f_y = -2x + 3y^2$; at $(1,1)$: $f_y = 1$.
  • Tangent plane: $z - 0 = 0(x-1) + 1(y-1)$, i.e., $z = y-1$.
  • Normal line: $\dfrac{x-1}{0} = \dfrac{y-1}{1} = \dfrac{z}{-1}$, i.e., $x=1$, $y = 1-t$, $z = t$. $\blacksquare$

Gradient Vector

The gradient of $f(x,y)$ is the vector

$$\nabla f = \text{grad}\, f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right) = f_x\,\mathbf{i} + f_y\,\mathbf{j}$$

For $f(x,y,z)$: $\nabla f = f_x\,\mathbf{i} + f_y\,\mathbf{j} + f_z\,\mathbf{k}$.

The gradient has two fundamental geometric meanings:

  1. $\nabla f(a,b)$ points in the direction of steepest ascent of $f$ at $(a,b)$.
  2. $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to level curves $f(x,y) = c$.

Directional Derivative

The directional derivative of $f$ at $(a,b)$ in the direction of the unit vector $\hat{u} = \langle \cos\theta, \sin\theta\rangle$ is

$$D_{\hat{u}}f(a,b) = \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{f(a+t\cos\theta,\, b+t\sin\theta) - f(a,b)}{t} = \nabla f(a,b)\cdot\hat{u}$$
$$D_{\hat{u}}f = |\nabla f|\cos\alpha \quad\text{where $\alpha$ is the angle between $\nabla f$ and $\hat{u}$}$$

The directional derivative is maximised when $\hat{u}$ is parallel to $\nabla f$ (maximum rate of increase = $|\nabla f|$), and minimised when $\hat{u}$ is anti-parallel (maximum rate of decrease = $-|\nabla f|$).

Example 3.3 — Directional Derivative

Find the directional derivative of $f(x,y) = x^2y + 3y^2$ at $(1,1)$ in the direction of $\vec{v} = \langle 2, -1\rangle$.

  • $f_x = 2xy = 2(1)(1) = 2$; $\quad f_y = x^2 + 6y = 1 + 6 = 7$. So $\nabla f(1,1) = \langle 2, 7\rangle$.
  • Unit vector: $\hat{u} = \dfrac{\langle 2,-1\rangle}{\sqrt{4+1}} = \left\langle\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{5}}, \dfrac{-1}{\sqrt{5}}\right\rangle$.
  • $D_{\hat{u}}f = \langle 2,7\rangle\cdot\left\langle\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{5}},\dfrac{-1}{\sqrt{5}}\right\rangle = \dfrac{4-7}{\sqrt{5}} = \dfrac{-3}{\sqrt{5}}$. $\blacksquare$

Level Curves and Level Surfaces

A level curve of $f(x,y)$ is the set $\{(x,y) : f(x,y) = c\}$ for a constant $c$. Level curves are the contours seen on topographic maps. The gradient $\nabla f$ at any point is perpendicular to the level curve through that point.

A level surface of $f(x,y,z)$ is $\{(x,y,z) : f(x,y,z) = c\}$. $\nabla f$ is the normal vector to this surface.

Section 04

Total Derivative

The total derivative generalises the concept of the derivative to functions of several variables. It captures the combined effect of small changes in all independent variables simultaneously.

The total differential of $z = f(x,y)$ is

$$dz = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,dy = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy$$

For $w = f(x,y,z)$: $\quad dw = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy + f_z\,dz$

$f(x,y)$ is differentiable at $(a,b)$ if there exist functions $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2$ such that

$$\Delta z = f_x\Delta x + f_y\Delta y + \varepsilon_1\Delta x + \varepsilon_2\Delta y$$

where $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2 \to 0$ as $(\Delta x, \Delta y) \to (0,0)$. Equivalently, the function has a linear approximation at $(a,b)$:

$$f(a+\Delta x, b+\Delta y) \approx f(a,b) + f_x(a,b)\Delta x + f_y(a,b)\Delta y$$
The existence of partial derivatives does NOT guarantee differentiability. A function can have partial derivatives everywhere but fail to be differentiable. However, if $f_x$ and $f_y$ are continuous at $(a,b)$, then $f$ is differentiable there.

Approximate Calculations Using Total Differential

For small increments $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$:

$$\Delta z \approx dz = f_x\,\Delta x + f_y\,\Delta y$$
Example 4.1 — Small Change

Find the approximate change in $z = x^3 y^2$ when $x$ changes from $2$ to $2.01$ and $y$ changes from $3$ to $2.98$.

  • $\Delta x = 0.01$, $\Delta y = -0.02$; base point $(2,3)$.
  • $f_x = 3x^2y^2 = 3(4)(9) = 108$; $\quad f_y = 2x^3 y = 2(8)(3) = 48$.
  • $dz = 108(0.01) + 48(-0.02) = 1.08 - 0.96 = 0.12$.
  • So $z$ increases by approximately $0.12$. $\blacksquare$
Example 4.2 — Error Propagation

The volume of a cylinder is $V = \pi r^2 h$. If $r$ is measured as $5$ cm with error $\pm 0.03$ cm, and $h$ as $10$ cm with error $\pm 0.05$ cm, find the maximum possible error in $V$.

  • $dV = \dfrac{\partial V}{\partial r}\,dr + \dfrac{\partial V}{\partial h}\,dh = 2\pi rh\,dr + \pi r^2\,dh$
  • $= 2\pi(5)(10)(0.03) + \pi(25)(0.05) = 3\pi + 1.25\pi = 4.25\pi \approx 13.35\text{ cm}^3$. $\blacksquare$
Example 4.3 — Relative Error

For $f(x,y) = x^m y^n$, show that the relative error in $f$ is $m\,\dfrac{dx}{x} + n\,\dfrac{dy}{y}$.

  • $df = mx^{m-1}y^n\,dx + nx^m y^{n-1}\,dy$
  • $\dfrac{df}{f} = m\cdot\dfrac{dx}{x} + n\cdot\dfrac{dy}{y}$ $\blacksquare$
Section 05

Total Derivative and Implicit Functions

The implicit function theorem allows us to find derivatives of functions defined implicitly by equations, without first solving for the dependent variable explicitly.

Suppose $F(x,y) = 0$ defines $y$ implicitly as a differentiable function of $x$ near a point $(a,b)$ with $F(a,b)=0$. If $F_y(a,b)\neq 0$, then

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y} = -\frac{\partial F/\partial x}{\partial F/\partial y}$$
Proof

Differentiating $F(x,y)=0$ totally with respect to $x$:

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\cdot 1 + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y} \qquad (F_y \neq 0)$$
□

If $F(x,y,z) = 0$ defines $z$ implicitly as a function of $x$ and $y$, and $F_z \neq 0$, then

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{F_x}{F_z}, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = -\frac{F_y}{F_z}$$
Example 5.1

Find $dy/dx$ given $x^3 + y^3 - 3axy = 0$.

  • Let $F(x,y) = x^3 + y^3 - 3axy$.
  • $F_x = 3x^2 - 3ay$, $\quad F_y = 3y^2 - 3ax$.
  • $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{3x^2 - 3ay}{3y^2 - 3ax} = -\dfrac{x^2 - ay}{y^2 - ax} = \dfrac{ay - x^2}{y^2 - ax}$. $\blacksquare$
Example 5.2

If $F(x,y,z) = x^2+y^2+z^2 - xyz = 0$, find $\partial z/\partial x$.

  • $F_x = 2x - yz$, $\quad F_z = 2z - xy$.
  • $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\dfrac{F_x}{F_z} = -\dfrac{2x - yz}{2z - xy} = \dfrac{yz - 2x}{2z - xy}$. $\blacksquare$
Example 5.3

If $u = f(x,y)$ and $y = g(x)$ (so $u$ ultimately depends only on $x$), find $du/dx$.

$$\frac{du}{dx} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}$$

This is the chain rule for a function of two variables where one depends on the independent variable.

Example 5.4 — Second Implicit Derivative

Given $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$, find $d^2y/dx^2$.

  • $F(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - r^2$; $F_x = 2x$, $F_y = 2y$, so $y' = -x/y$.
  • $y'' = \dfrac{d}{dx}\!\left(-\dfrac{x}{y}\right) = -\dfrac{y - x\,y'}{y^2} = -\dfrac{y - x(-x/y)}{y^2} = -\dfrac{y^2+x^2}{y^3} = -\dfrac{r^2}{y^3}$. $\blacksquare$
Section 06

Euler's Theorem on Homogeneous Functions

Homogeneous functions arise naturally in economics (returns to scale), physics (dimensional analysis), and geometry. Euler's theorem provides an elegant characterisation of these functions via their partial derivatives.

A function $f(x,y)$ is said to be a homogeneous function of degree $n$ if for every $t > 0$:

$$f(tx, ty) = t^n f(x,y)$$

Equivalently, $f(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \lambda^n f(x,y)$ for all scalars $\lambda$.

Examples: $f(x,y) = x^3 + x^2 y + y^3$ (degree 3); $f(x,y) = \dfrac{x^2+y^2}{x+y}$ (degree 1); $f(x,y) = \sin(x/y)$ (degree 0).

If $f(x,y)$ is a homogeneous function of degree $n$ with continuous first partial derivatives, then

$$x\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + y\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = n\,f(x,y)$$
Proof of Euler's Theorem

Since $f$ is homogeneous of degree $n$, we have $f(tx,ty) = t^n f(x,y)$ for all $t>0$.

Differentiate both sides with respect to $t$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}[f(tx,ty)] = \frac{d}{dt}[t^n f(x,y)]$$

Left side (chain rule): $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial(tx)}\cdot x + \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial(ty)}\cdot y$.

Right side: $nt^{n-1}f(x,y)$.

Setting $t=1$:

$$x\,f_x(x,y) + y\,f_y(x,y) = n\,f(x,y)$$
□

If $f(x,y)$ is homogeneous of degree $n$ with continuous second partial derivatives, then

$$x^2\,f_{xx} + 2xy\,f_{xy} + y^2\,f_{yy} = n(n-1)\,f$$
Derivation of the Corollary

From Euler's theorem: $x\,f_x + y\,f_y = n\,f$. Since $f_x$ is homogeneous of degree $(n-1)$, applying Euler's theorem to $f_x$:

$$x\,f_{xx} + y\,f_{yx} = (n-1)\,f_x \quad\cdots(1)$$

Applying Euler's theorem to $f_y$ (homogeneous of degree $n-1$):

$$x\,f_{xy} + y\,f_{yy} = (n-1)\,f_y \quad\cdots(2)$$

Multiply (1) by $x$ and (2) by $y$, then add:

$$x^2 f_{xx} + xy\,f_{yx} + xy\,f_{xy} + y^2 f_{yy} = (n-1)(xf_x + yf_y) = (n-1)\cdot nf$$

Using $f_{xy} = f_{yx}$ (Clairaut): $x^2 f_{xx} + 2xy\,f_{xy} + y^2 f_{yy} = n(n-1)f$. $\blacksquare$

□

If $f(x,y,z)$ is homogeneous of degree $n$, then

$$x\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + y\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} + z\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = n\,f$$

Worked Examples — Euler's Theorem

Example 6.1

Verify Euler's theorem for $f(x,y) = x^3 + 3x^2y + 3xy^2 + y^3 = (x+y)^3$.

  • Degree $n=3$. $f_x = 3x^2 + 6xy + 3y^2$; $f_y = 3x^2 + 6xy + 3y^2$. Wait — let us compute carefully.
  • $f_x = 3x^2 + 6xy + 3y^2$, $f_y = 3x^2 + 6xy + 3y^2$.
  • $xf_x + yf_y = x(3x^2+6xy+3y^2)+y(3x^2+6xy+3y^2) = (x+y)(3x^2+6xy+3y^2) = 3(x+y)^3 = 3f$. ✓
Example 6.2

If $u = \dfrac{x^4+y^4}{x+y}$, find the value of $x\,u_x + y\,u_y$.

  • Check homogeneity: $u(tx,ty) = \dfrac{t^4x^4+t^4y^4}{t(x+y)} = t^3\,u$. So degree $n=3$.
  • By Euler's theorem: $xu_x + yu_y = 3u = \dfrac{3(x^4+y^4)}{x+y}$. $\blacksquare$
Example 6.3

If $u = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{x^2+y^2}{x+y}\right)$, prove that $xu_x + yu_y = \tan u$.

  • Let $v = \sin u = \dfrac{x^2+y^2}{x+y}$. Check: $v$ is homogeneous of degree 1 in $x,y$.
  • By Euler's theorem applied to $v$: $xv_x + yv_y = 1\cdot v = v = \sin u$.
  • Since $v = \sin u$, $v_x = \cos u\cdot u_x$ and $v_y = \cos u\cdot u_y$.
  • Thus $\cos u\,(xu_x + yu_y) = \sin u \implies xu_x + yu_y = \dfrac{\sin u}{\cos u} = \tan u$. $\blacksquare$
Example 6.4 — Verify the Corollary

For $f(x,y) = x^2 + xy + y^2$ (degree 2), verify $x^2 f_{xx} + 2xy f_{xy} + y^2 f_{yy} = n(n-1)f$.

  • $f_x = 2x+y$, $f_y = x+2y$, $f_{xx}=2$, $f_{xy}=1$, $f_{yy}=2$.
  • $x^2(2)+2xy(1)+y^2(2) = 2x^2+2xy+2y^2 = 2(x^2+xy+y^2) = 2(1)f = n(n-1)f$ with $n=2$: $n(n-1)=2$. ✓
Example 6.5 — Composite Homogeneous

If $f(x,y) = \ln\!\left(\dfrac{x^3+y^3}{x-y}\right)$, find $x\,f_x + y\,f_y$.

  • Let $g = \dfrac{x^3+y^3}{x-y}$; then $g(tx,ty) = t^2 g$, so $g$ is homogeneous of degree 2.
  • $f = \ln g$, so $f_x = g_x/g$ and $f_y = g_y/g$.
  • $xf_x + yf_y = (xg_x + yg_y)/g = 2g/g = 2$. $\blacksquare$
Section 07

Chain Rule for Functions of Several Variables

The chain rule extends the single-variable rule to multivariable settings, telling us how to differentiate composite functions where the intermediate variables themselves depend on parameters.

If $u = f(x,y)$ is differentiable, and $x = g(t)$, $y = h(t)$ are differentiable functions of $t$, then

$$\frac{du}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dt}$$

If $u = f(x,y)$, $x = g(s,t)$, $y = h(s,t)$, then

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial s} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\cdot\frac{\partial x}{\partial s} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{\partial y}{\partial s}$$ $$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\cdot\frac{\partial x}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}$$
Tree Diagram Technique: Draw a tree: $u$ at top, branches to $x$ and $y$ (labelled $\partial u/\partial x$, $\partial u/\partial y$), then branches from $x$ to $s,t$ and from $y$ to $s,t$ labelled $\partial x/\partial s$, etc. Each path from $u$ to a leaf gives a product of partial derivatives; the chain rule sum collects all such products.
Example 7.1

If $u = x^2 + y^2$, $x = t^2$, $y = e^t$, find $du/dt$.

  • $\partial u/\partial x = 2x = 2t^2$; $\quad dx/dt = 2t$.
  • $\partial u/\partial y = 2y = 2e^t$; $\quad dy/dt = e^t$.
  • $\dfrac{du}{dt} = 2t^2\cdot 2t + 2e^t\cdot e^t = 4t^3 + 2e^{2t}$. $\blacksquare$
Example 7.2 — Chain Rule with Two Parameters

If $z = e^{xy}$, $x = s^2 t$, $y = s/t$, find $\partial z/\partial s$ and $\partial z/\partial t$.

  • $\partial z/\partial x = ye^{xy}$, $\partial z/\partial y = xe^{xy}$.
  • $\partial x/\partial s = 2st$, $\partial y/\partial s = 1/t$.
  • $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial s} = ye^{xy}\cdot 2st + xe^{xy}\cdot\dfrac{1}{t} = e^{xy}\!\left(2sty + \dfrac{x}{t}\right) = e^{s^2} \!\left(2s^2 + \dfrac{s^2}{t^2}\cdot t\right)$. Substitute $xy = s^2t\cdot s/t = s^3$: $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial s} = e^{s^3}\!\left(2s\cdot\frac{s}{t}\cdot t + \frac{s^2t}{t}\right) = e^{s^3}(2s^2 + s^2) = 3s^2 e^{s^3}$.
  • Similarly $\partial z/\partial t$ can be computed.
Example 7.3 — Three-Variable Chain Rule

If $w = f(x,y,z)$, $x = r\sin\phi\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\phi\sin\theta$, $z = r\cos\phi$ (spherical coordinates), write the chain rule for $\partial w/\partial r$.

$$\frac{\partial w}{\partial r} = f_x\sin\phi\cos\theta + f_y\sin\phi\sin\theta + f_z\cos\phi$$
Section 08

Jacobians

The Jacobian determinant is a fundamental tool in multivariable calculus. It measures how much a transformation stretches or compresses area/volume, and it appears crucially in change-of-variable formulas for multiple integrals.

If $u = u(x,y)$ and $v = v(x,y)$ are functions of $x$ and $y$, the Jacobian of $(u,v)$ with respect to $(x,y)$ is the determinant

$$J = \frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)} = \begin{vmatrix}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}\\[10pt] \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial y}\end{vmatrix} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\cdot\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$$

For three functions $u=u(x,y,z)$, $v=v(x,y,z)$, $w=w(x,y,z)$, the Jacobian is the $3\times 3$ determinant:

$$\frac{\partial(u,v,w)}{\partial(x,y,z)} = \begin{vmatrix} u_x & u_y & u_z \\ v_x & v_y & v_z \\ w_x & w_y & w_z\end{vmatrix}$$

Properties of Jacobians

Chain Rule for Jacobians: If $(u,v)$ is a function of $(x,y)$ and $(x,y)$ is a function of $(r,s)$, then

$$\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(r,s)} = \frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\cdot\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,s)}$$

Inverse Transformation: If $J = \partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)$ and $J' = \partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)$, then

$$J\cdot J' = 1 \quad\text{(when the transformation is invertible)}$$

$u$ and $v$ are functionally dependent (i.e., one is a function of the other) if and only if

$$\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)} = 0 \quad\text{identically}$$

Worked Examples — Jacobians

Example 8.1 — Polar Coordinates

Find the Jacobian $\partial(x,y)/\partial(r,\theta)$ for the transformation $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$.

$$J = \begin{vmatrix}\cos\theta & -r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta\end{vmatrix} = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r$$

This confirms the familiar area element formula: $dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$. $\blacksquare$

Example 8.2 — Spherical Coordinates

For $x = r\sin\phi\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\phi\sin\theta$, $z = r\cos\phi$, find $\partial(x,y,z)/\partial(r,\phi,\theta)$.

$$J = \begin{vmatrix} \sin\phi\cos\theta & r\cos\phi\cos\theta & -r\sin\phi\sin\theta\\ \sin\phi\sin\theta & r\cos\phi\sin\theta & r\sin\phi\cos\theta\\ \cos\phi & -r\sin\phi & 0\end{vmatrix} = r^2\sin\phi$$

This gives the volume element $dV = r^2\sin\phi\,dr\,d\phi\,d\theta$. $\blacksquare$

Example 8.3 — Jacobian Computation

If $u = x^2 - y^2$ and $v = 2xy$, find $\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)$.

$$J = \begin{vmatrix} 2x & -2y \\ 2y & 2x\end{vmatrix} = 4x^2 + 4y^2 = 4(x^2+y^2) \neq 0 \text{ (away from origin)}$$

So $u$ and $v$ are functionally independent. $\blacksquare$

Example 8.4 — Functional Dependence

Check if $u = x+y+z$, $v = x^2+y^2+z^2-2(xy+yz+zx)$, $w = x^3+y^3+z^3-3xyz$ are functionally dependent.

Compute $\partial(u,v,w)/\partial(x,y,z)$. One can verify that $v = (x+y+z)^2 - 4(xy+yz+zx)$ and $w = u^3 - 3u(xy+yz+zx)+3xyz$, showing $v$ and $w$ can be expressed as functions of $u$ and elementary symmetric polynomials. The Jacobian determinant equals $0$ identically, confirming functional dependence.

Example 8.5 — Inverse Jacobian

For $u = e^x\cos y$, $v = e^x\sin y$, verify that $J\cdot J' = 1$.

  • $J = \partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y) = \begin{vmatrix}e^x\cos y & -e^x\sin y \\ e^x\sin y & e^x\cos y\end{vmatrix} = e^{2x}\cos^2y + e^{2x}\sin^2 y = e^{2x}$.
  • The inverse: $x = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(u^2+v^2)$, $y = \tan^{-1}(v/u)$.
  • $J' = \partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v) = e^{-2x} = 1/e^{2x}$, so $J\cdot J' = 1$. ✓
Section 09

Taylor's Series for Two Variables

Taylor's theorem generalises from one variable to two, expressing a smooth function as a power series in increments from a base point. This is essential for local analysis, approximations, and the second derivative test.

If $f(x,y)$ has continuous partial derivatives of all orders in a neighborhood of $(a,b)$, and $h = x-a$, $k = y-b$, then

$$f(a+h, b+k) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(h\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+k\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)^n f(a,b)$$

Explicitly, up to second-order terms:

$$f(a+h,b+k) = f(a,b) + (hf_x + kf_y) + \frac{1}{2!}(h^2 f_{xx} + 2hk\,f_{xy} + k^2 f_{yy}) + \cdots$$

where all partial derivatives are evaluated at $(a,b)$.

The symbolic operator $(h\partial/\partial x + k\partial/\partial y)^n$ is expanded using the binomial theorem and applied term by term to $f$ at $(a,b)$.

Setting $a=b=0$ (expand about the origin), $h=x$, $k=y$:

$$f(x,y) = f(0,0) + (xf_x + yf_y)_0 + \frac{1}{2!}(x^2f_{xx}+2xy\,f_{xy}+y^2 f_{yy})_0 + \frac{1}{3!}(x^3 f_{xxx}+3x^2y\,f_{xxy}+3xy^2 f_{xyy}+y^3 f_{yyy})_0 + \cdots$$

Worked Examples — Taylor Expansions

Example 9.1 — Expand $e^x\cos y$ about $(0,0)$ up to 3rd degree
  • Build a table of values at $(0,0)$:
$f$ and DerivativesValue at $(0,0)$
$f = e^x\cos y$$1$
$f_x = e^x\cos y$$1$
$f_y = -e^x\sin y$$0$
$f_{xx} = e^x\cos y$$1$
$f_{xy} = -e^x\sin y$$0$
$f_{yy} = -e^x\cos y$$-1$
$f_{xxx} = e^x\cos y$$1$
$f_{xxy} = -e^x\sin y$$0$
$f_{xyy} = -e^x\cos y$$-1$
$f_{yyy} = e^x\sin y$$0$

Therefore:

$$e^x\cos y = 1 + x + \frac{1}{2!}(x^2 - y^2) + \frac{1}{3!}(x^3 - 3xy^2) + \cdots$$ $$= 1 + x + \frac{x^2-y^2}{2} + \frac{x^3 - 3xy^2}{6} + \cdots \quad\blacksquare$$
Example 9.2 — Expand $e^{x+y}$ about $(0,0)$

Note that $e^{x+y} = e^x\cdot e^y$. Using Maclaurin:

$$e^{x+y} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(x+y)^n}{n!} = 1 + (x+y) + \frac{(x+y)^2}{2!} + \frac{(x+y)^3}{3!}+\cdots$$ $$= 1 + x+y + \frac{x^2+2xy+y^2}{2} + \frac{x^3+3x^2y+3xy^2+y^3}{6}+\cdots\quad\blacksquare$$
Example 9.3 — Expand $\sin(xy)$ up to 3rd degree terms
  • $f = \sin(xy)$, $f(0,0)=0$.
  • $f_x = y\cos(xy)$, $f_x(0,0)=0$. $f_y = x\cos(xy)$, $f_y(0,0)=0$.
  • $f_{xx} = -y^2\sin(xy)=0$, $f_{xy} = \cos(xy)-xy\sin(xy)=1$ at origin. $f_{yy}=-x^2\sin(xy)=0$.
  • The second-degree contribution: $\dfrac{1}{2!}\cdot 2\cdot(1)\cdot xy = xy$.
  • For third-degree terms, all third partials at origin are zero.
  • Therefore: $\sin(xy) = xy - \dfrac{(xy)^3}{3!} + \cdots \approx xy$ to third degree. $\blacksquare$
Example 9.4 — Taylor's Series about $(1,1)$

Expand $f(x,y) = xy + \ln(xy)$ about the point $(1,1)$ up to second-order terms.

  • $f(1,1) = 1+0 = 1$.
  • $f_x = y + 1/x$; $f_x(1,1) = 2$. $f_y = x + 1/y$; $f_y(1,1) = 2$.
  • $f_{xx} = -1/x^2$; at $(1,1)$: $-1$. $f_{yy} = -1/y^2$; at $(1,1)$: $-1$. $f_{xy} = 1$; at $(1,1)$: $1$.
  • Let $h = x-1$, $k = y-1$:
$$f(x,y) \approx 1 + 2h + 2k + \frac{1}{2!}(-h^2 + 2hk - k^2) = 1+2(x-1)+2(y-1) - \frac{(x-1)^2-2(x-1)(y-1)+(y-1)^2}{2}\quad\blacksquare$$
Standard Taylor Series Reference
  • $e^{x+y} = \displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(x+y)^n}{n!}$
  • $\sin(x+y) = (x+y) - \dfrac{(x+y)^3}{6} + \cdots$
  • $\cos(x+y) = 1 - \dfrac{(x+y)^2}{2} + \cdots$
  • $\ln(1+x+y) \approx (x+y) - \dfrac{(x+y)^2}{2}+\cdots$
Section 10

Extrema of Functions of Two Variables

Finding maxima and minima of $f(x,y)$ — and distinguishing them from saddle points — is a central problem in optimisation. The theory uses the second-order Taylor expansion and the Hessian determinant.

$f$ has a local maximum at $(a,b)$ if $f(x,y)\leq f(a,b)$ for all $(x,y)$ near $(a,b)$.

$f$ has a local minimum at $(a,b)$ if $f(x,y)\geq f(a,b)$ for all $(x,y)$ near $(a,b)$.

$(a,b)$ is a saddle point if it is a critical point but neither a local max nor a local min — $f$ increases in some directions and decreases in others.

If $f$ has a local extremum at an interior point $(a,b)$ and the partial derivatives exist there, then

$$f_x(a,b) = 0 \quad\text{and}\quad f_y(a,b) = 0$$

Such points are called critical points (stationary points) of $f$.

Let $(a,b)$ be a critical point of $f$ with continuous second partial derivatives. Define the Hessian discriminant:

$$H = f_{xx}(a,b)\cdot f_{yy}(a,b) - \bigl[f_{xy}(a,b)\bigr]^2$$
  • $H > 0$ and $f_{xx}(a,b) > 0$: $(a,b)$ is a local minimum.
  • $H > 0$ and $f_{xx}(a,b) < 0$: $(a,b)$ is a local maximum.
  • $H < 0$: $(a,b)$ is a saddle point.
  • $H = 0$: inconclusive — higher-order analysis needed.
Derivation of the Second Derivative Test

Near a critical point $(a,b)$, by Taylor's theorem with $h = x-a$, $k=y-b$:

$$f(a+h,b+k) - f(a,b) \approx \frac{1}{2}\!\left[f_{xx}h^2 + 2f_{xy}hk + f_{yy}k^2\right]$$

(The first-order terms vanish since $f_x=f_y=0$.) Write the quadratic form as $Q(h,k) = Ah^2+2Bhk+Ck^2$ where $A=f_{xx}$, $B=f_{xy}$, $C=f_{yy}$. Complete the square in $h$:

$$Q = \frac{1}{A}\!\left[(Ah+Bk)^2 + (AC-B^2)k^2\right]$$

If $AC-B^2 > 0$ and $A>0$: both squares contribute positively, $Q>0$ always (except at origin), so $f$ has a local minimum. If $A<0$: $Q<0$ always, so $f$ has a local maximum. If $AC-B^2 < 0$: $Q$ changes sign (saddle). $\blacksquare$

□

Worked Examples — Finding Extrema

Example 10.1

Find and classify all critical points of $f(x,y) = x^3 + y^3 - 3xy$.

  • Set $f_x = 3x^2 - 3y = 0$ and $f_y = 3y^2 - 3x = 0$, giving $y = x^2$ and $x = y^2$.
  • Substituting: $x = (x^2)^2 = x^4 \implies x^4 - x = 0 \implies x(x^3-1)=0$, so $x=0$ or $x=1$.
  • Critical points: $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$.
  • $f_{xx}=6x$, $f_{xy}=-3$, $f_{yy}=6y$.
  • At $(0,0)$: $H = (0)(0)-9 = -9 < 0$ ⟹ saddle point.
  • At $(1,1)$: $H = (6)(6)-9 = 27 > 0$ and $f_{xx}=6>0$ ⟹ local minimum, $f(1,1)=1+1-3=-1$. $\blacksquare$
Example 10.2

Find the maximum and minimum values of $f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - 2x - 4y + 8$.

  • $f_x = 2x-2 = 0 \implies x=1$; $f_y = 2y-4=0 \implies y=2$. Critical point: $(1,2)$.
  • $f_{xx}=2$, $f_{xy}=0$, $f_{yy}=2$. $H = 4-0 = 4 > 0$, $f_{xx}=2>0$.
  • Local minimum at $(1,2)$: $f(1,2) = 1+4-2-8+8 = 3$. $\blacksquare$

Note: Since $f = (x-1)^2+(y-2)^2+3 \geq 3$, this is actually the global minimum.

Example 10.3

Find all critical points of $f(x,y) = 4xy - x^4 - y^4$ and classify them.

  • $f_x = 4y - 4x^3 = 0 \implies y = x^3$. $f_y = 4x - 4y^3 = 0 \implies x = y^3$.
  • From $y=x^3$ and $x=y^3$: $x = (x^3)^3 = x^9 \implies x(x^8-1)=0$, so $x=0,\,1,-1$.
  • Critical points: $(0,0)$, $(1,1)$, $(-1,-1)$.
  • $f_{xx}=-12x^2$, $f_{xy}=4$, $f_{yy}=-12y^2$.
  • At $(0,0)$: $H = 0\cdot 0 - 16 = -16 < 0$ ⟹ saddle point.
  • At $(1,1)$: $H = (-12)(-12) - 16 = 128 > 0$, $f_{xx}=-12 < 0$ ⟹ local maximum, $f(1,1)=4-1-1=2$.
  • At $(-1,-1)$: Same as $(1,1)$ by symmetry ⟹ local maximum, $f(-1,-1)=2$. $\blacksquare$
Example 10.4 — Finding Global Extrema on a Closed Region

Find the absolute maximum and minimum of $f(x,y) = xy$ on the closed region $D = \{(x,y): x^2+y^2\leq 1\}$.

  • Interior critical points: $f_x = y = 0$, $f_y = x = 0 \implies (0,0)$. $f(0,0)=0$.
  • Boundary: On $x^2+y^2=1$, let $x=\cos\theta$, $y=\sin\theta$. Then $f = \cos\theta\sin\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta$.
  • Maximum of $\tfrac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta$: at $\theta=\pi/4$ (giving $x=y=1/\sqrt{2}$), $f=1/2$.
  • Minimum: at $\theta=3\pi/4$, $f=-1/2$.
  • Absolute maximum = $1/2$; Absolute minimum = $-1/2$. $\blacksquare$
Section 11

Lagrange's Method of Undetermined Multipliers

Lagrange multipliers provide an elegant method for solving constrained optimisation problems — maximising or minimising an objective function subject to one or more constraint equations — without the need to eliminate variables explicitly.

To find the extrema of $f(x,y,z)$ subject to the constraint $g(x,y,z) = 0$, solve the system:

$$\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g$$

i.e., the three equations $f_x = \lambda g_x$, $f_y = \lambda g_y$, $f_z = \lambda g_z$, together with the constraint $g=0$. The constant $\lambda$ is called the Lagrange multiplier.

Geometric Derivation

At a constrained extremum, the level surface $f=c$ is tangent to the constraint surface $g=0$. Tangency means they share the same tangent plane, which means their normals are parallel. Since $\nabla f$ is normal to level surfaces of $f$ and $\nabla g$ is normal to the constraint surface:

$$\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g \quad\text{for some scalar $\lambda$.}$$
□

To extremise $f(x,y,z)$ subject to $g(x,y,z)=0$ and $h(x,y,z)=0$:

$$\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g + \mu\,\nabla h$$

giving the system $f_x=\lambda g_x+\mu h_x$, $f_y=\lambda g_y+\mu h_y$, $f_z=\lambda g_z+\mu h_z$, plus both constraints.

Worked Examples — Lagrange Multipliers

Example 11.1 — Rectangular Box of Maximum Volume

Find the dimensions of a rectangular box of maximum volume such that the sum of its edges is $12a$ (i.e., $4(x+y+z) = 12a$, so $x+y+z = 3a$).

  • Maximise $V = xyz$ subject to $g = x+y+z-3a = 0$.
  • $\nabla V = \lambda\nabla g$: $(yz, xz, xy) = \lambda(1,1,1)$, so $yz = xz = xy = \lambda$.
  • From $yz = xz$: $y=x$ (since $z\neq 0$). From $xz = xy$: $z=y$. So $x=y=z$.
  • From constraint: $3x = 3a \implies x=a$.
  • Maximum volume: $V = a^3$ at $x=y=z=a$ (cube). $\blacksquare$
Example 11.2 — Closest Point on a Plane to the Origin

Find the point on the plane $2x + y + 2z = 9$ closest to the origin.

  • Minimise $f = x^2+y^2+z^2$ subject to $g = 2x+y+2z-9=0$.
  • $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$: $(2x,2y,2z) = \lambda(2,1,2)$.
  • $x = \lambda$, $y = \lambda/2$, $z = \lambda$.
  • Constraint: $2\lambda + \lambda/2 + 2\lambda = 9 \implies (9/2)\lambda = 9 \implies \lambda = 2$.
  • Closest point: $(2, 1, 2)$, with distance $\sqrt{4+1+4}=3$. $\blacksquare$
Example 11.3 — Two Constraints

Find the maximum value of $f = xy + yz$ subject to $y^2 = xz$ and $x+y+z = a$.

  • Let $g = y^2 - xz = 0$ and $h = x+y+z-a = 0$.
  • $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g + \mu\nabla h$:
  • $f_x = y = \lambda(-z)+\mu(1)$, so $y = -\lambda z + \mu$.
  • $f_y = x+z = \lambda(2y)+\mu$.
  • $f_z = y = \lambda(-x)+\mu$, so $y = -\lambda x + \mu$.
  • From equations 1 and 3: $-\lambda z + \mu = -\lambda x + \mu \implies z=x$.
  • From $y^2=xz=x^2 \implies y=x$ (taking positive root). From $x+x+x=a$: $x=a/3$.
  • So $x=y=z=a/3$ and $f = xy+yz = (a/3)^2+(a/3)^2 = 2a^2/9$. $\blacksquare$
Example 11.4 — Minimum Distance from a Point to an Ellipse

Find the points on the ellipse $x^2/4 + y^2/9 = 1$ closest and farthest from the origin.

  • Extremise $f = x^2+y^2$ subject to $g = x^2/4+y^2/9-1 = 0$.
  • $2x = \lambda(x/2)$ and $2y = \lambda(2y/9)$, giving $\lambda = 4$ or $x=0$, and $\lambda = 9$ or $y=0$.
  • If $x\neq 0$ and $y\neq 0$: $\lambda=4$ and $\lambda=9$ simultaneously — impossible.
  • Case $x=0$: constraint gives $y^2=9$, $y=\pm 3$. $f=9$.
  • Case $y=0$: constraint gives $x^2=4$, $x=\pm 2$. $f=4$.
  • Closest points: $(\pm 2, 0)$ at distance $2$. Farthest: $(0,\pm 3)$ at distance $3$. $\blacksquare$
Example 11.5 — Production Optimisation

A firm's production function is $Q = 5L^{2/3}K^{1/3}$, where $L$ = labour and $K$ = capital. The cost constraint is $2L + 3K = 120$. Find the values of $L$ and $K$ that maximise output.

  • Maximise $Q = 5L^{2/3}K^{1/3}$ subject to $g = 2L+3K-120=0$.
  • $Q_L = \dfrac{10}{3}L^{-1/3}K^{1/3} = 2\lambda$; $\quad Q_K = \dfrac{5}{3}L^{2/3}K^{-2/3} = 3\lambda$.
  • Dividing: $\dfrac{Q_L}{Q_K} = \dfrac{10K/3}{5L/3} = \dfrac{2K}{L} = \dfrac{2}{3} \implies \dfrac{K}{L} = \dfrac{1}{3} \implies K = L/3$.
  • Constraint: $2L + 3(L/3) = 2L+L = 3L = 120 \implies L = 40$, $K = 40/3$.
  • Max output: $Q = 5(40)^{2/3}(40/3)^{1/3} = 5\cdot 40\cdot 3^{-1/3} = \dfrac{200}{3^{1/3}}\approx 144.2$ units. $\blacksquare$
Example 11.6 — Box with Maximum Volume and Fixed Surface Area

Find the dimensions of a closed rectangular box with maximum volume and surface area $S = 150$ cm².

  • Maximise $V = xyz$ subject to $2(xy+yz+xz) = 150$, i.e., $g = xy+yz+xz-75 = 0$.
  • $yz = \lambda(y+z)$, $xz = \lambda(x+z)$, $xy = \lambda(x+y)$.
  • By symmetry of the equations, suppose $x=y=z$. Then $g = 3x^2-75=0 \implies x=5$.
  • The box is a cube of side $5$ cm, $V_{\max} = 125$ cm³. $\blacksquare$
Key Takeaways — Lagrange Multipliers
  • The method converts a constrained problem into solving a system of algebraic equations.
  • The multiplier $\lambda$ has an economic interpretation: it is the marginal value of relaxing the constraint.
  • The method finds candidates — always verify by comparing values or using second-order conditions.
  • For $n$ variables and $m$ constraints, we need $\nabla f = \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i$ — yielding $n+m$ equations for $n+m$ unknowns.
  • By symmetry: if $f$ and $g$ are symmetric in variables and a symmetric solution exists, it is often the extremum.
